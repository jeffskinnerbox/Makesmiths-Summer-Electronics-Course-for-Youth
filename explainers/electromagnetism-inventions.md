# Electromagnetism's Inventions: From a Twitching Compass Needle to Fusion Magnets
In Class 5 you wind 100 turns of wire around a screwdriver, touch the ends to a 9-volt battery, and a paper
clip jumps to the tip (Experiment 25). You press a button and watch one LED flash as a coil's magnetic field
builds, and a second LED flash as it collapses (Experiment 28). In Class 6 you shuttle a neodymium magnet
through a coil of wire and light an LED with electricity you generated yourself — no battery (Experiment 26).
Those three bench-top moments are not toy versions of the modern world. They **are** the modern world, at
one-millionth scale. Nearly every machine that moves, every watt on the grid, every sound from a speaker, and
— for half a century — every bit your computer stored, comes from exactly the relationship you just held in
your hands.

What is the answer to these questions:

1. What exactly did humanity discover in 1820 and 1831, and why did those two discoveries change everything?
1. How does one two-way relationship — current makes magnetism, and a moving magnet makes current — turn into
   motors, generators, telephones, loudspeakers, and computer memory?
1. Which magnet-and-coil inventions, from the telegraph to MRI scanners and fusion reactors, carried that
   relationship from its discovery to the present day — and what did each one give society?

The short answer: in 1820 [Hans Christian Ørsted][01] noticed a compass needle twitch beside a
current-carrying wire — **electricity creates magnetism** — and in 1831 [Michael Faraday][03] in England and
[Joseph Henry][04] in America independently found the reverse — **a changing magnetic field creates
electricity**. Everything since is engineering built on those two sentences. Humanity has spent two centuries
exploiting the same two-way street you cross in Classes 5 and 6, first to send *signals* (telegraph,
telephone), then to move *power* (generators, motors, transformers), then to carry *sound and information*
(speakers, radio, magnetic recording), and now to reach the frontier (MRI, maglev, fusion). Let's take the
whole story apart.


## 1. Overview
Electricity and magnetism are two faces of a single phenomenon, **electromagnetism**, connected by a two-way
relationship. One way: current flowing through a wire wraps the wire in a magnetic field; coil the wire and
add an iron core, and the field concentrates into a strong, **switchable** magnet — the electromagnet of
Experiment 25. The other way: move a magnet through a coil (or a coil through a field) and the changing field
pushes electrons through the wire — **electromagnetic induction**, the hand generator of Experiment 26. A
coil also stores energy in its own field and returns it as a pulse when the current stops — the
collapsing-field flash of Experiment 28.

Every electromagnetic invention in the two-century timeline below is one of a handful of exploitations of
that relationship: use the field's **pull** to do work at a distance (telegraphs, relays, solenoids, cranes);
reverse the field rhythmically to make **continuous rotation** (motors); drive the relationship backward to
**generate power** (dynamos, alternators, wind turbines); couple two coils through one field to **transform
and transmit power** (the AC grid); or let the field **carry information** (telephones, loudspeakers,
magnetic tape, hard disks, MRI). Input a current or a motion; output a force, a voltage, a sound, or a stored
bit. The machinery of modern civilization is variations on the theme you build on the bench in Classes 5
and 6.


## 2. Detailed Operation
How does one relationship become two hundred years of machines? Trace the chain of exploitation, in the order
humanity discovered each link.

### Step 1: A Current Wraps Itself in a Magnetic Field
The trigger for everything came in 1820, when [Ørsted][01] noticed that a compass needle jumped whenever he
connected a nearby wire to a battery. The effect is invisible but always present: any flowing current creates
a magnetic field circling the wire. The field from a single straight wire is feeble — far too weak to do
useful work — which is why this step alone built nothing but laboratory curiosities and one crucial
instrument, the **galvanometer** (1820), which used the twitch of a magnetized needle to *measure* current
and made electrical science quantitative. The problem this step hands to Step 2 is concentration: the effect
is real, but it is spread out and weak.

### Step 2: Coils and Iron Concentrate the Field into a Usable Force
Bend the wire into a loop, and the field from every part of the loop adds up through the center; stack a
hundred loops into a coil, and it adds a hundred times; slide iron into the core, and the iron multiplies the
effect again. William Sturgeon built the first true [electromagnet][02] on this principle in 1825 — seven
ounces of iron lifting nine pounds — and [Joseph Henry][04] scaled it within a few years to magnets lifting
over a thousand pounds. This is Experiment 25's screwdriver, industrialized. The output of this step is a
**strong magnetic pull that can be switched on and off with a current**, and that switchable pull is the raw
material for Step 3.

### Step 3: A Switched Field Does Work at a Distance
A switchable pull means a current *here* can move a piece of iron *there* — and "there" can be at the end of
a very long wire. Close a switch in Washington, and an electromagnet in Baltimore clicks: that is the
[telegraph][08] (1844), the first invention to separate information from transportation. Let the
electromagnet's pull close a second, separate circuit, and you have Henry's **relay** (1835) — a switch
operated by electricity, which re-amplified telegraph signals across a continent and, a century later,
computed inside the first digital computers (see [Electricity and Computation][19]). Let the pull yank a
plunger and you have the **solenoid** — door locks, valves, the starter solenoid in a car. Let it grab tons
of scrap steel and you have the junkyard **crane magnet**. Every one of these is Experiment 25's paper-clip
jump, given a job. The limitation is that a simple electromagnet pulls in straight, single strokes — Step 4
turns the pull into continuous motion.

### Step 4: A Reversing Field Produces Continuous Rotation — the Motor
Reverse the current through a coil and its magnetic poles swap. Arrange coils and magnets on a shaft, and
switch the current at just the right moments (with a mechanical commutator, or with electronics in a modern
brushless motor), and the magnetic pull never gets to finish — the rotor chases a field that keeps flipping
ahead of it, forever. That is an [electric motor][05]: continuous rotation from a steady electrical supply.
From Thomas Davenport's patented curiosity (1837) through Tesla's brushless **induction motor** (1888) to the
neodymium-magnet motors in drones and electric cars, the motor put controllable mechanical power anywhere a
wire could reach — trams, elevators, factory machines, power tools, washing machines, robots. A motor,
however, consumes electricity, and in the 1830s electricity still came from expensive chemical batteries.
Step 5 removes that bottleneck.

### Step 5: A Moving Magnet Pushes Current — the Generator
Run the relationship backward. In 1831 [Faraday][03] showed that *moving* a magnet relative to a coil —
changing the magnetic field through it — induces a voltage in the coil. Spin magnets past coils continuously
and you have a [generator][06]: mechanical work in, electric current out, for as long as something keeps
spinning. Water wheels, steam engines, and eventually steam *turbines* (Parsons, 1884) provided the spin, and
generation scaled from Pixii's hand-cranked machine (1832) to Niagara Falls (1895) to gigawatt power
stations. This is Experiment 26 exactly — your shuttled magnet and 200-foot coil, scaled up — and it is still
how nearly every watt on Earth's grid is made: hydro, coal, gas, nuclear, and wind differ only in what spins
the magnets (see [Every Type of Electric Generator Explained][17]). Cheap, continuous current then feeds
every previous step — and makes Step 6 both possible and necessary.

### Step 6: Two Coils Sharing One Field Move Power Across Continents
Generated power is worthless if it can't reach customers, and low-voltage current loses too much energy as
heat in long wires (as you saw in *Why Do Wires Heat Up?*). The fix is a device with no moving parts at all:
the [transformer][07] (1885) — two coils wound on one iron core. Alternating current in the first coil makes
a continuously *changing* field in the core, and by induction that changing field drives current in the
second coil, at a voltage set by the ratio of turns. Step voltage up 100:1 for the journey, down again at the
neighborhood — this single trick is why AC won the "War of the Currents" (see
[The War of the Currents][18]) and why a power plant can serve customers a hundred miles away. The same
coil-to-coil coupling, miniaturized, is the wireless charger under a phone and the induction cooktop that
heats a steel pan directly. With power now abundant and delivered everywhere, Step 7 puts the relationship to
its subtlest use.

### Step 7: The Field Carries Sound, Signals, and Stored Information
A magnetic field can follow a *signal*, not just a switch. Let a voice-shaped current flow through a coil
attached to a paper cone sitting in a permanent magnet's field, and the cone vibrates in the shape of the
voice — the loudspeaker (1925), and, run in reverse, the dynamic microphone; the same reciprocity as
motor/generator, applied to sound. Bell's [telephone][08] (1876) did this first, with an electromagnet
driving an iron diaphragm. Let the signal-shaped field magnetize a passing steel wire or oxide-coated tape,
and the recording *stays* — [magnetic storage][09] (1898), which became tape, the hard disk (1956), core
memory that flew to the Moon, the cassette, and the striped credit card; a hard drive still writes bits with
a microscopic electromagnet today. And a field changing fast enough detaches from the wire entirely and
travels as a **radio wave** — the leap Maxwell predicted on paper (see [Displacement Current][20] and
[Maxwell's Equations][21]) and Marconi turned into wireless telegraphy (1901). Signal, storage, and radio
closed the loop: electromagnetism now moved power *and* meaning, and the timeline in Section 6 shows what
humanity built with that.

## 4. Key Parameters
The story of exploitation is largely a story of *field strength* and *scale*. Magnetic field strength is
measured in **teslas (T)**.

| Parameter | Typical Value | Notes |
| :---------- | :--------------- | :------ |
| Earth's magnetic field | ~0.00005 T | What a compass needle follows — and all Ørsted had to work with |
| Refrigerator magnet | ~0.005 T | About 100× Earth's field |
| Sturgeon's electromagnet (1825) | lifted ~9 lb | Seven ounces of iron and one battery — the first switchable magnet |
| Henry's electromagnets (early 1830s) | lifted ~1,000-2,000 lb | Same principle, more turns of insulated wire `[VERIFY]` exact lift figures vary by source |
| Neodymium magnet (1984) | ~1.4 T at surface | Strongest permanent magnets made; enabled small motors, earbuds, drones |
| MRI scanner | 1.5-3 T | Superconducting electromagnet, always on, wrapped around the patient |
| LHC bending magnets (2008) | 8.3 T | 1,232 superconducting dipoles chilled to −271 °C |
| CFS/MIT fusion magnet (2021) | 20 T | High-temperature superconductor; strong enough to bottle a fusion plasma |
| Your Class 6 hand generator | ~0.8 V AC | 200 ft of wire + shuttled magnet (meter-averaged; peaks higher) |
| Grid turbo-generator | ~20,000 V, hundreds of MW | The identical principle, with steam or water doing the shuttling |


## 5. Common Misconceptions
- **Misconception:** "Electricity and magnetism are two separate phenomena that happen to interact."
  **Reality:** They are one phenomenon — **electromagnetism**. A changing electric current *always* makes a
  magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field *always* pushes on charges. Maxwell's equations describe both
  as aspects of a single field, which is why one bench-top relationship could spawn every device in the
  timeline.

- **Misconception:** "A permanent magnet is an energy source — motors get free energy from their magnets."
  **Reality:** Magnets supply *force and field*, never net energy. A motor's energy comes from the electric
  current; a generator's comes from whatever moves the magnet (your arm, steam, wind). Stop paying in current
  or motion and the machine stops — a generator is an energy *converter*, not an energy *container*.

- **Misconception:** "Electromagnets are 19th-century technology that semiconductors replaced."
  **Reality:** Chips took over *computing*, but essentially everything that physically moves, makes sound, or
  generates power is still a magnet and a coil. A modern smartphone carries a dozen electromagnetic devices —
  speaker, microphones, haptic motor, camera-stabilizing voice coils, wireless-charging coil — and virtually
  every watt that charges it was generated by a spinning magnet.

- **Misconception:** "The coil in Experiment 28 stores electricity like a little battery."
  **Reality:** The coil stores energy in its **magnetic field**, only while current flows, and returns it in
  a split-second pulse when the field collapses. That pulse — nuisance enough to need a diode across a relay
  coil — was itself turned into inventions: the ignition coil that sparks a gasoline engine is a deliberately
  harvested collapsing-field pulse.


## 6. Why this matters everywhere: two centuries of magnet-and-coil inventions
The exploitation came in waves, each wave standing on the last. First an **age of signals** (1820-1875):
batteries were weak, so the earliest inventions used tiny currents to move tiny pieces of iron —
galvanometers, telegraphs, relays, bells — and the payoff was instantaneous long-distance communication,
something no amount of pre-electric engineering could buy. Then an **age of power** (1875-1900): dynamos,
transformers, motors, and the AC grid made energy itself a utility piped into buildings, and electric light,
trams, and factory motors rebuilt cities around it. Then an **age of sound and machines** (1900-1950):
loudspeakers, radio, magnetic recording, car starters and ignition coils, household appliance motors, radar
magnetrons — electromagnetism entered the home, the car, and the kitchen. Then an **age of information**
(1950-2000): core memory, hard disks, tape, and magnetic stripes made the magnet the world's memory, while
motors shrank into every machine around you. And now a **frontier age** (2000-present): superconducting
electromagnets image your brain, levitate 600 km/h trains, steer particle beams, and — if fusion succeeds —
may bottle a star. One relationship, five waves, and the table below: the most impactful inventions humanity
has built from electricity plus a magnet or electromagnet, from the discovery to today.

Read down the table and the pattern of Section 2 repeats like a drumbeat: pull (telegraph, relay, crane),
rotation (motor, tram, drone), induction (dynamo, Niagara, wind turbine), transformation (grid, cooktop,
wireless charger), information (telephone, tape, hard disk, MRI). Not one entry required a new physical
principle beyond the two discoveries of 1820 and 1831 — two centuries of civilization-scale invention drawn
from the relationship that fits on your breadboard.


## Bottom line
In 1820 a compass needle twitched beside a wire, and in 1831 a moving magnet pushed a current through a coil
— and those two observations are the *entire* physical capital behind the timeline above. Humanity exploited
the relationship in five escalating waves: switched magnetic **pull** gave us the telegraph, relay, and
solenoid; rhythmically reversed pull gave us the **motor**; the relationship run backward gave us the
**generator** and the power grid; two coils sharing a field gave us the **transformer** and the AC world; and
a field shaped by a signal gave us the telephone, the loudspeaker, magnetic recording, and radio — later
scaled by superconductivity into MRI machines, maglev trains, particle colliders, and fusion magnets. Every
one of the ~60 inventions in the table contains the same two ingredients you used in Classes 5 and 6:
electricity, and a magnet or electromagnet. When your screwdriver grabs the paper clip, you are Sturgeon in
1825; when your released button flashes the second LED, you are firing a tiny ignition coil; and when your
shuttled magnet lights an LED with no battery, you are running Niagara Falls between your finger and thumb.
