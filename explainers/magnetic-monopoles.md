# Magnetic Monopoles: Why a Magnet Never Runs Down — and Never Comes Apart
In Classes 5 and 6 (Experiments 25, 26, and 28) you worked both sides of electromagnetism: you wound wire
around a screwdriver and a 9-volt battery turned it into a magnet; you disconnected a coil and its collapsing
field flashed an LED; you moved a neodymium magnet through a coil and generated your own electricity. Along
the way you leaned on two very different "natural sources": a **battery**, whose chemistry pushes electrons
around a circuit, and a **permanent magnet**, which simply *is* magnetic, no wires required. Comparing the
two raises two excellent questions:

1. A battery's chemistry eventually runs its course, the electrons stop flowing, and the battery is dead.
   Does a natural magnet ever lose its magnetic field the same way?
1. You can find a positively charged object or a negatively charged object all by itself — you never need
   the pair. But magnets always have a north pole *and* a south pole, together, every time. Why can't you
   ever find just a north or just a south?

The short answer: a magnet is not a fuel tank, so it cannot "run out" the way a battery does — but its
magnetism is a kind of *order*, and order can be destroyed by heat, shock, or an opposing field. And poles
always come in pairs because a magnetic pole is not a *thing* the way an electric charge is a thing:
electric charge is a property that particles carry, while magnetism is something moving charge *does*.
Every magnet, all the way down to a single electron, is really a tiny loop of circulating charge — and a
loop always has two faces. A lone pole would be one face of a loop without the other, and nobody has ever
found one. Physicists even have a name for that missing object — the **magnetic monopole** — and they are
still hunting for it. Let's take it apart.


## 1. Overview
A **permanent magnet** produces a persistent magnetic field with no power source because the magnetism is
built into its atoms. Each electron acts as a minuscule magnet (an effect of its **spin**, which behaves
like electric charge circulating in a tiny loop). In **ferromagnetic** materials — iron, nickel, cobalt,
and alloys like the neodymium magnet's Nd₂Fe₁₄B — neighboring atoms lock their tiny magnets into alignment,
forming microscopic aligned patches called **domains**. In raw iron the domains point every which way and
cancel; magnetizing the material means forcing the domains to point the same way, and a "hard" magnetic
material holds that alignment after the magnetizing field is removed. The output is the familiar dipole
field: closed loops that leave the north face, curve around to the south face, and return *through* the
magnet. Because the field is stored alignment rather than stored fuel, using a magnet consumes nothing —
but heating it past its **Curie temperature**, hammering it, or bucking it with a strong opposing field
scrambles the alignment and the magnetism is gone.


## 2. Detailed Operation

### Step 1: Every Electron Is Already a Magnet
Magnetism does not need a second ingredient beyond the electricity you already know. A charge moving in a
loop creates a magnetic field — that is exactly why your 100-turn coil in Experiment 25 became a magnet —
and every electron carries a built-in version of this called **spin**. Spin makes each electron behave like
an impossibly small current loop with a fixed magnetic strength (about 9.3 × 10⁻²⁴ joules per tesla, a
quantity called the Bohr magneton). Crucially, even this smallest magnet in nature is a **dipole**: it has
a north face and a south face, because a loop of circulation always has two sides. These atomic-scale
magnets are the raw material for Step 2.

### Step 2: Exchange Coupling Lines Up Atoms into Domains
In most materials the electron magnets pair off and cancel, which is why wood and plastic aren't magnetic.
In ferromagnetic materials — iron, nickel, cobalt, and magnetite (the mineral in a natural lodestone) — some
electron magnets are left unpaired, and a quantum effect called **exchange coupling** makes it energetically
cheaper for neighboring atoms to point the same way. The result is that alignment happens spontaneously in
microscopic patches called **magnetic domains**, each roughly a micrometer across and containing something
like 10¹⁵ atoms all pointing together. In an ordinary iron nail these domains exist right now — but they
point in random directions, so their fields cancel and the nail as a whole is not a magnet. This
randomly-ordered state is the input to Step 3.

### Step 3: An External Field Aligns the Domains
Magnetizing a material means applying a strong external field that bullies the domains into agreement:
domains already pointing roughly the right way grow at the expense of their neighbors (the walls between
domains physically move through the crystal), and stubborn domains rotate. In a **soft** magnetic material
like your screwdriver's steel shaft, the alignment mostly relaxes when the field is removed — which is why
the screwdriver stopped being a strong magnet when you disconnected the battery. In a **hard** magnetic
material like Nd₂Fe₁₄B, the crystal structure locks each domain's direction in place, so the alignment
survives after the factory's magnetizing pulse ends. A natural lodestone got its alignment the same way —
magnetized by the Earth's field and, in the strongest specimens, likely by the intense field of a lightning
strike. This frozen-in alignment is what Step 4 turns into the field you can feel.

### Step 4: Aligned Loops Sum into One Big Dipole
With all domains pointing the same way, the countless atomic current loops add up like coil turns — your
two 50-turn coils in Experiment 25 aiding each other, but a trillion-fold. Inside the material, neighboring
loops cancel each other's sides, leaving the equivalent of one large circulation flowing around the outside
of the magnet. The resulting field emerges from one face (which we *label* north), arcs around, enters the
opposite face (labeled south), and — this is the key point — continues **through the body of the magnet**
back to the north face. Every field line is a closed loop with no beginning and no end. This is why cutting
a magnet in half can never separate the poles: each half still contains its own swarm of complete atomic
loops, so each half instantly presents a fresh north and south face. You don't get a separated north and a
separated south — you get two smaller, complete magnets, every time, all the way down to a single electron.
Physics writes this rule as Gauss's law for magnetism (one of Maxwell's four equations): the net magnetic
"charge" in any volume is always exactly zero.

### Step 5: Holding On Consumes Nothing
Here is where the battery comparison breaks. A battery is a chemical fuel tank: every electron it pushes
through your LED is paid for by converting reactants (zinc, manganese dioxide, lithium compounds) into
products, and when the reactants are spent, the pumping stops. A magnet holding a paper clip to your
refrigerator is doing something different: it did work once, briefly, while *pulling the clip in*, and from
then on it holds the clip the way a hook holds a coat — a static force, no energy flowing, nothing being
consumed. The field is not fuel being burned; it is alignment being maintained, and maintaining alignment
in a hard magnetic material costs nothing. This is why a magnet has no expiration date from use — and why
the only real threats to it are the alignment-scramblers in Step 6.

### Step 6: How the Order Can Be Destroyed
A permanent magnet can still "die" — not by running out, but by having its order destroyed. **Heat** is the
big one: atoms in a warm solid constantly jiggle, and above a critical temperature — the **Curie
temperature**, 770 °C for iron, roughly 310–400 °C for neodymium-magnet alloy — the jiggling overwhelms
exchange coupling, the domains dissolve, and the magnetism vanishes. (Cool it back down with no field
present and the domains re-form in random directions: a demagnetized magnet, though it can be re-magnetized.)
Well below the Curie point, heat still weakens neodymium magnets — ordinary grades start losing strength
permanently above roughly 80 °C, a genuine design limit for motors. **Mechanical shock** — hammering or
repeatedly dropping a magnet — jars domains out of alignment a few at a time. A **strong opposing field**
can flip domains wholesale; a material's resistance to this is called its coercivity, and it is what
separates "hard" magnets from "soft" ones. Absent all of these, the decay is astonishingly slow: a modern
neodymium magnet kept at room temperature loses well under 1% of its strength per century `[VERIFY]` —
compare that to the months-to-years shelf life of the battery chemistry it was riding alongside on your
breadboard.

## 5. Common Misconceptions
- **Misconception:** "A magnet slowly drains as it picks things up, like a battery running down."
  **Reality:** Holding a static load transfers no energy, so use costs a magnet nothing. A battery converts
  stored chemicals into electron flow and is consumed; a magnet is stored *alignment*, and only heat, shock,
  or an opposing field — not use — destroys it.

- **Misconception:** "If you cut a magnet in half, one piece gets the north pole and the other gets the south."
  **Reality:** Each half instantly has both poles, because each half still contains complete atomic current
  loops. Halve it forever, down to one electron, and you still hold a two-poled dipole.

- **Misconception:** "Permanent magnetism and electromagnetism are two different phenomena."
  **Reality:** They are the same phenomenon. Your Experiment 25 coil and a neodymium magnet both get their
  field from circulating charge — the coil from electrons moving through wire, the magnet from electron spin
  aligned across trillions of atoms.

- **Misconception:** "Scientists found magnetic monopoles in 2009 — I read the headlines."
  **Reality:** Those experiments found monopole-*like* ripples inside crystals called spin ices — emergent
  quasiparticles that mimic free poles within the material. They are real and fascinating, but no isolated
  fundamental monopole has ever been detected in nature.


## 6. Why this matters everywhere: the hunt for the missing monopole
The deep answer to your second question is an inventory of what the universe is made of. Electric charge is
a **property of particles**: an electron simply *has* minus one unit of charge, a proton plus one, and you
can bottle either one alone. But nature appears to have issued no matching particle that *has* magnetic
charge. All the magnetism we have ever observed is a side effect of electric charge in motion, and motion
in a loop has two faces you cannot pry apart. That absence is written into the foundations of physics:
Maxwell's equations, the four rules governing all of electricity and magnetism, contain a term for electric
charge but a hard zero where magnetic charge would go — and that missing term is the *only* asymmetry
spoiling their otherwise beautiful electric–magnetic symmetry.

That itch has kept physicists searching for nearly a century. In 1931 [Paul Dirac][04] proved something
remarkable: if even **one** magnetic monopole exists anywhere in the universe, quantum mechanics *requires*
electric charge to come in fixed steps — which it does (every electron's charge is exactly the same), a fact
nothing else cleanly explains. Later "grand unified" theories predicted that monopoles were mass-produced in
the Big Bang. So detectors went hunting: on Valentine's Day 1982, a Stanford experiment run by Blas Cabrera
recorded a single, perfect monopole-shaped signal — never repeated, never explained, and never confirmed.
Today the dedicated [MoEDAL experiment][05] at CERN's Large Hadron Collider keeps watch for monopoles made
in particle collisions, and so far the count remains zero. In science, a clean *absence* like this is
valuable: "no monopoles" is a measured fact about our universe that any future theory has to explain.

Meanwhile, the domain physics behind your *first* question quietly runs the modern world. Every hard drive
stores its bits as tiny magnetized regions — writing data literally means flipping domain alignments with a
miniature electromagnet, the same act as magnetizing your screwdriver, billions of times per second. The
neodymium magnets in electric-car motors, wind-turbine generators, headphones, and phone speakers are all
Experiment 26 scaled up, and their ~80 °C weakening point is a real constraint motor designers engineer
around with cooling and special high-temperature grades. Demagnetization gets used on purpose, too: in
World War II, ships were "degaussed" — their hulls' magnetic alignment deliberately scrambled with huge
coils — to hide from magnetic mines, and the same idea securely erases data from old hard drives today.

And the "natural sources" you compared have a shared punchline. The lodestone that gave humanity the
compass is just magnetite whose domains were aligned by the Earth's own field — and the Earth is not a
permanent magnet at all. Its core is far above iron's Curie temperature, so no alignment can survive there;
the planet's field comes instead from churning currents of molten iron — a colossal, self-sustaining
electromagnet. Deep down there is only one phenomenon in this story, seen from two sides: charge in motion
makes magnetism, and changing magnetism moves charge — exactly the two-way handshake your coils,
batteries, and magnets demonstrated on the breadboard.


## Bottom line
A battery dies because it is a fuel tank: its chemistry converts stored reactants into electron flow, and
when the reactants are gone, so is the current. A permanent magnet is not a fuel tank — it is stored
*order*, trillions of electron-sized current loops locked into alignment — so using it consumes nothing and
it never "runs out"; but heat past the Curie point, hard shocks, or a strong opposing field can scramble
that order and kill the field (after which it can be re-magnetized, something no dead battery can offer).
And poles come in pairs because magnetism has no particle of its own: electric charge is a thing particles
*have*, while magnetism is a thing moving charge *does* — and circulation always has two faces. Cut any
magnet as small as you like and every piece keeps a north and a south, because every piece still contains
complete loops. The hypothetical exception — a **magnetic monopole**, a bare north or south — would rewrite
Maxwell's equations and explain why charge comes in fixed steps, which is why physicists have hunted it
from the Big Bang's leftovers to CERN's collider without finding a single one. For more, see
[Magnetic monopole][01], [Magnetic domain][02], [Curie temperature][03], [Paul Dirac][04], the
[MoEDAL experiment][05], and [Lodestone][06].
